## $${\color{red}UI \ UX \ - \ Useful \ Concepts}$$

Interesting facts, concepts, assumptions, researches, concepts and more from the world of UI UX. <br>
Only helpful wisdom for practical use in design.

- Unit bias - cognitive tendency to view a single portion or item—regardless of its actual size—as the appropriate amount to consume or complete. This heuristic leads people to overconsume, such as eating a whole large sandwich instead of half, because they perceive "one" unit as the proper serving, often driving increased food intake or purchasing behavior.

- Hick’s Law - statement in design that the time it takes for a person to make a decision increases with the number and complexity of choices. To improve User Experience (UX), designers minimize options, break down complex processes (like checkouts) into steps, and highlight recommended choices to reduce cognitive overload and prevent user fatigue.

- CTB - Call to Benefit - marketing technique that replaces a traditional, action-oriented command (CTA) with a focus on the value, outcome, or "why" a user should engage. Instead of just telling the audience what to do (e.g., "Sign up"), a CTB explains what they will gain (e.g., "Join to get exclusive tips").

- availability bias - cognitive shortcut (heuristic) where people overestimate the likelihood of events based on how easily examples come to mind, often favoring recent, vivid, or emotionally charged information over objective data. It causes flawed, non-rational decision-making in areas like investing, risk assessment, and medicine.

- Gulf of Execution - gap between a user’s goals and the physical actions required by a system to achieve them. It measures how easily a user can understand and operate controls. A large gap causes frustration, while a small gap enables intuitive, efficient, and seamless interaction.

- Gulf of Evaluation - difficulty a user experiences when trying to interpret the state of a system and determine if their previous actions successfully achieved their goals. Coined by Donald Norman, it represents the gap between a user's desired outcome and the feedback (or lack thereof) provided by the interface.

- nonverbal communication - transmission of messages, emotions, and attitudes without words, often subconsciously, through cues like facial expressions, body language, gestures, eye contact, and tone of voice. It strengthens, complements, or replaces verbal communication to build rapport, express emotions, and manage social impressions.

- Nielsen's heuristics - 10 general principles of digital interface design based on the problems real users face when using digital systems. Importantly, Nielsen's heuristics are not hard and fast guidelines, but general rules of thumb.

- rule of thumb - practical principle or guideline that can be used as a rough basis for making decisions or solving problems. Rules of thumb are often based on experience or observations, and they can be useful in situations where exact calculations are not necessary or possible.

- natural mapping - design principle in user interface (UI) and human-computer interaction (HCI) that creates an intuitive relationship between controls and their actions, allowing users to interact with systems immediately without instructions. By using spatial, physical, or conceptual analogies, it reduces cognitive load and accelerates, making actions feel natural.

- Stimulus-response (S-R) compatibility - degree to which the relationship between a stimulus (e.g., a visual cue) and its required response (e.g., a physical action) aligns with human expectations or "natural" mappings. High compatibility, such as turning a knob clockwise to increase volume, results in faster, more accurate, and intuitive performance.

- friction - In web design is an obstacle—such as slow loading speeds, confusing navigation, or complex checkout processes—that frustrates users and hinders them from completing actions, leading to lower conversions, higher bounce rates, and reduced brand loyalty. It includes technical issues like broken links, poor mobile design, or excessive, unnecessary form fields.

- Low-friction/-commitment CTA - low-commitment, conversational prompt designed to initiate engagement without overwhelming the prospect, often yielding higher response rates in cold emails and landing pages. Examples include "Worth exploring?""Open to a quick back and forth?", or "Mind if I send a 60-second video?".

- High-friction/-commitment CTA - phrases or design elements that require a high commitment, investment, or effort from users, such as "Buy Now" "Submit" or "Sign Up". These CTAs create psychological or physical obstacles—often perceived as chores or financial risk—that can slow down or stop conversions.

- Paradox of Choice - psychological concept stating that an abundance of options leads to cognitive overload, resulting in decision paralysis, anxiety, and lower satisfaction with the final choice. While more choices are traditionally seen as greater freedom, too many alternatives make deciding harder and increase regret.

- IA - information architecture - structural design, organization, and labeling of content in digital products (websites, apps) to support usability, navigation, and findability. It acts as a blueprint, structuring, organizing, and labeling content to ensure users can easily find information, ultimately improving user experience (UX) and navigation efficiency.
